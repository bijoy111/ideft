What Moving a Replica Actually Costs

The online analysis turns on one number: M, the cost of migrating a replica expressed in rounds of ordinary replication. We had been assuming M = 3. This page measures it against a real network-function state table and finds M = 23–41 — an order of magnitude larger, and large enough to overturn one of our own earlier conclusions.

real connection-tracking table Poisson arrivals, Pareto lifetimes validated by Little's law three wire encodings
Why this could not be measured before. DEFT's backup receives state and discards it — the persistence code is commented out, and update_state does nothing but print. So there was no state, no table, and no size to transfer. To get a number we built a real one: a connection-tracking / NAT flow table keyed on the 5-tuple, with incremental delta replication, TCP state tracking and idle eviction, driven by a flow-arrival workload.

Is the workload realistic?

Before trusting any size, check the generator. Little's law predicts steady-state occupancy as arrival rate × (mean lifetime + idle timeout), independently of how the table is implemented:

Agreement within 3.3%. This is a check on the generator, not a second result.

DEFT's wire encoding costs 7.3×

The same table, serialized three ways. xmlrpc is what DEFT actually puts on the wire.

A correctness finding, not just a size one. XML-RPC's <int> is a signed 32-bit integer. An IPv4 address of 128.0.0.0 or above does not fit, and encoding one raises an overflow. Any NAT mapping on the upper half of the address space cannot be sent as an integer at all and must go as text — which, with the per-value markup, is where most of the 7.31× comes from.

The migration cost itself

M is the time to ship the whole table divided by the time to replicate one round's delta. At 2,000 flows/s:

EncodingLinkFull migrationService / roundM (rounds)
packed1 Gb/s184.9 ms4.71 ms39.2
packed25 Gb/s163.9 ms4.01 ms40.9
json10 Gb/s342.5 ms9.15 ms37.4
xmlrpc10 Gb/s2143.4 ms92.53 ms23.2
M barely depends on the encoding or the link. That looks wrong at first and is the most useful thing here. A costlier encoding inflates the full transfer and the per-round delta by the same factor, so the ratio is nearly unchanged. M tracks 1 / delta-share — roughly 1/3.3% ≈ 30 — which is a property of how fast the traffic churns, not of how you serialize it or how fast your NIC is. You cannot engineer M away by picking a better wire format.
What this corrected. With the assumed M = 3, migrating away from a degraded backup looked cheap and our degradation-aware policy looked worthwhile. At the measured M = 23–41 the round trip — leave, then come back when the host recovers — costs 2M, and degradation has to last a long time before that pays. We revised the conclusion rather than the measurement. The ordering is stable at the top of the measured range; at the bottom it is close enough that we state it precisely rather than rounding in our favour.
