Online: When Hosts Change While You Watch

Every other page on this site answers a one-shot question: given the cluster as it is right now, which backup should each primary use? Real clusters do not hold still. Hosts degrade, recover and die, and moving a replica is not free — you have to ship the state. This page asks what adaptation is actually worth, and the honest answer is less than you would expect.

16 hosts, 10 primaries, horizon 300 10 seeds per point exact offline optimum by DP competitive-ratio analysis
The setup. Each round, every host's health, capacity and latency drift; a two-state Markov chain degrades and recovers hosts, and a smaller rate kills them outright. A policy pays a service cost each round for the backup it is using, and a migration cost M every time it moves one. We compare against the exact offline optimum — computed by dynamic programming over the time×host trellis, so it is a real lower bound and not another heuristic.

Re-solving every round is unboundedly bad

Theorem 1. Let snapshot be the policy that plays a cost-minimizing assignment for round t at every t — exactly what this paper's Hungarian optimal baseline would do if you deployed it and re-ran it each round. For every migration cost M and every ρ, there is an instance on which snapshot costs more than ρ times the optimum. It is not ρ-competitive for any ρ.

This is not a worst-case curiosity bolted on after the fact. Two hosts alternate as the better choice by a vanishing margin ε; snapshot chases the margin and pays M each time, while the optimum stands still and pays ε per round. We verified the construction empirically — the measured ratio tracks 1/ε exactly, reaching 5940×:

What adaptation is actually worth

Four policies, measured as excess over the offline optimum (1.00 = as good as the optimum), at the most volatile churn setting with hard failures enabled:

PolicyWhat it doesExcess ratio
staticnever moves42.25
failovermoves only when a host dies4.64
hysteresisfull degradation-aware migration4.59
snapshotre-solves every roundunbounded
The main result is negative, and we report it that way. Adding failover alone to a static policy closes 91.2% of the distance to the offline optimum. Adding full degradation-aware hysteresis on top of that closes a further 0.13 percentage points. Nearly all the value of adapting to churn comes from the simplest possible rule — move when the host dies — and very little from sophisticated degradation tracking. This mirrors what the bare-metal measurement found for the one-shot problem: the cheap policy captures almost everything.

A certified migration budget

A hysteresis policy that only migrates when the accumulated regret exceeds α·M cannot spend more than 1/α of its service cost on migration. That is a proof, not an observation, and it holds at every threshold we measured:

Budget used stays under the bound at every α, with the tightest observation at roughly half the permitted spend. The practical reading: you can pick a migration budget in advance and the policy will respect it, which is what an operator actually needs.

Why the migration cost matters so much. Every number here depends on M, the cost of moving a replica measured in rounds of ordinary replication. The original model assumed M = 3. The migration-cost page measures it against a real connection-tracking table and finds M = 23–41. That correction overturned an earlier conclusion of ours about when migration pays.
