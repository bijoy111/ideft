\begin{abstract}
Fault-tolerant Network Function Virtualization (NFV) systems replicate a primary
network function's state to a backup instance, but none of them specify how that
backup instance is chosen. We close that gap, and in doing so show that
evaluating such a choice is considerably harder than it appears.

We design three selection strategies --- a capacity-constrained Hungarian
assignment used as an attainable bound, a multi-criteria greedy heuristic, and a
reinforcement-learning policy --- and evaluate them against a deployed system's
unmodified two-phase-commit backup at four topology scales, over two RPC
transports, and on a bare-metal testbed where 128 backups each hold a dedicated
physical core and degraded hosts are impaired by the kernel rather than by
simulation.

Three measurement results generalize beyond this system. A connection-per-call
RPC stack with a short accept queue biases measured placement effects with a sign
that depends on workload concentration: it inflated a concentration penalty
$7.5\times$ at 128 hosts while understating a placement gap at 16. Marking a
contiguous block of hosts as degraded lets an ordering-based baseline appear to
avoid an impairment it never encounters. And re-solving the instantaneous optimum
every round, the natural deployment of an exact one-shot method, is not
competitive for any ratio.

The placement result is robust under all of these. Feature-aware selection reaches
a degraded backup in $0\%$ of calls against a $10$--$12.5\%$ base rate at every
scale, roughly halving p99, and a greedy heuristic is statistically
indistinguishable from the exact assignment throughout.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Network Function Virtualization (NFV) replaces dedicated hardware middleboxes with
virtualized network functions (NFs) running on commodity servers, enabling elastic
scaling and fault tolerance that fixed hardware cannot provide~\cite{han2015network}.
A stateful NF's correctness depends on preserving per-flow and global state across
failures and load-driven scaling events. The dominant approach to fault tolerance for
stateful NFs is \emph{replication}: a primary NF instance forwards its state updates to
one or more backup (secondary) instances, which take over processing if the primary
fails~\cite{rajagopalan2013pico,sherry2015rollback,kulkarni2018reinforce}. DEFT~\cite{shahriyar2023deft}
extends this line of work with a distributed, controller-free two-phase-commit (2PC)
protocol that achieves both elastic scaling and fault tolerance with strong consistency
on global state updates, without the single point of failure a central controller
introduces.

\subsection{The Gap: Selection of Backup Nodes}

Across this entire line of work, backup assignment is never considered as a decision with consequences. REINFORCE~\cite{kulkarni2018reinforce} designates
a predecessor node and a backup node per NF without describing a selection rule.
OpenNF~\cite{gember2014opennf}, Split/Merge~\cite{rajagopalan2013split}, StatelessNF~\cite{kablan2017stateless}, and S6~\cite{woo2018elastic} all describe
how state is migrated or shared once a target instance is known, never how that
target is chosen in the first place. In DEFT's own reference implementation, this
manifests concretely: the shipped static mapping for its 9-host evaluation topology
concentrates 3 of 5 primaries onto a single backup host, with no load or health
awareness whatsoever~\cite{shahriyar2023deft}.

This is a consequential gap. The entire point of replication for fault tolerance is
that the backup must be available and capable of taking over. If the backup itself is
overloaded, unhealthy, or already serving many other primaries, the guarantees of the replication mechanism are only as good as an arbitrary assignment allows. Elastic
scaling compounds this: as NF instances are added and removed under varying load, a
backup pool's composition changes continuously, and a static or naively-assigned mapping
cannot track it. We deliberately scope this work to the fault-tolerance half of state
management, namely replication, because that is where the backup-selection decision lives.
Choosing a target for state migration during elastic scaling is a related but distinct
decision; our assignment formulation could extend to it, but we do not evaluate it here.

\subsection{Why the Answer Is Hard to Measure}

Closing the gap is straightforward to state: score candidate hosts and assign
accordingly. Establishing that a particular rule is better than an arbitrary one
is not, and that difficulty is the larger part of what this paper reports.

A placement experiment claims that a latency difference is caused by the choice
of backup. Three things routinely break that attribution, and all three produced
results in our own work that we had to withdraw before arriving at the ones
reported here.

The first is the RPC stack. Replication is carried over some transport, and if
that transport opens a connection per call and the server holds a short accept
queue, a degraded host's modest per-call penalty is converted into queue overflow
and a one-second retransmission wait. The resulting measurement is dominated by
connection setup. Worse, the distortion is not a constant: it scales with how
many primaries share a backup, so it inflates the apparent cost of concentration
at large scale while understating the gap between policies at small scale
(\S\ref{sec:p99}, \S\ref{sec:testbed}).

The second is how the experiment constructs a degraded host. A simulated delay
inside the server measures the model of degradation rather than degradation. And
if degraded hosts are chosen as a contiguous block of identifiers, a policy that
traverses hosts in order may never reach them, scoring a perfect avoidance rate
it did not earn (\S\ref{sec:testbed}).

The third is the horizon. Backup selection in operation is not a one-shot
decision: hosts degrade, recover and fail, and moving a replica costs a state
transfer. An exact one-shot method evaluated once says little about the policy
obtained by re-running it, which we show is not competitive for any ratio
(\S\ref{sec:online}).

Each of these is a property of how placement is evaluated rather than of this
system, and we report them as such.

\subsection{Contributions}

We address backup-node selection directly, using DEFT as the concrete system
under study, though the problem and the methods generalize to any primary/backup
replication scheme. Our contributions are of two kinds: a solution to the
selection problem, and a set of measurement results about how such solutions must
be evaluated.

\textbf{Measurement methodology.} Evaluating a placement policy means attributing
a latency difference to the choice of host. We show three ways that attribution
fails, each of which applies beyond this system:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The RPC stack biases the measured effect, and not in a fixed
direction} (\S\ref{sec:p99}, \S\ref{sec:testbed}). A connection-per-call
transport whose server holds a short accept queue converts a degraded host's
modest per-call penalty into accept-queue overflow and a one-second
retransmission wait. Holding every other variable fixed and changing only that
queue length, the measured concentration penalty falls from $7.5\times$ to
$35\%$ at 128 hosts, while at 16 hosts the same change \emph{widens} the gap
between placement methods. The bias tracks how concentrated the workload is, so
it cannot be corrected by a constant factor. Any placement study conducted over
connection-per-call RPC is subject to it.

\item \textbf{A contiguous impairment lets an ordering-based baseline appear to
avoid it} (\S\ref{sec:testbed}). Degrading a contiguous block of hosts and
reporting only latency, we measured a round-robin policy matching an exact
assignment while selecting a degraded host in $0\%$ of calls --- not by avoiding
them, but because its traversal order never reached them. Reporting what share of
each method's calls actually met the impairment is necessary for the comparison
to mean anything.

\item \textbf{Re-solving the instantaneous optimum every round is not
competitive for any ratio} (\S\ref{sec:online}). The natural way to deploy an
exact one-shot method is to re-run it as conditions change. We prove this has
unbounded competitive ratio against the offline optimum once migration costs
anything, and confirm the construction empirically. An exact method evaluated
one-shot therefore says little about its behaviour in operation.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Backup selection.} Against that measurement background, we:

\begin{enumerate}
\setcounter{enumi}{3}
\item Formulate backup selection as a feasibility- and load-aware assignment
problem and design three methods: an exact capacity-constrained assignment via
the Hungarian algorithm, used as an attainable bound rather than a deployment
candidate; a deterministic multi-criteria greedy heuristic; and a PPO policy with
invalid-action masking.

\item Evaluate them on a bare-metal testbed where the attribution holds: 128
backup servers across ten nodes, each pinned to a dedicated physical core with
the affinity verified rather than assumed, on a 25\,GbE LAN, with degraded hosts
impaired by \texttt{tc netem} on the server's own source port so the slowdown is
produced by the kernel and healthy co-tenants on the same interface are
untouched (\S\ref{sec:testbed}). Feature-aware selection reaches a degraded
backup in $0\%$ of calls at 16, 32 and 128 hosts against a $10$--$12.5\%$ base
rate for ordering-based selection, and the greedy heuristic is statistically
indistinguishable from the exact assignment at every scale.

\item Characterize when selection matters and when it does not. With homogeneous
hosts no method separates from random, with a median paired difference of
$0.002$\,ms ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}} = 1.000$); the
heuristic's advantage is specific to heterogeneous pools and grows with the pool.
A sensitivity analysis over the scoring weights and decay constants
(\S\ref{sec:weights}) shows which term carries the guarantee, and that one
failure kind --- a host degraded only in its network path --- defeats the published
scoring function until latency is measured rather than derived from topology.

\item Report the measured behaviour of reinforcement learning on this problem
without overstating it (\S\ref{sec:rl128}, \S\ref{sec:dqn}). PPO matches the
feature-aware methods to 32 hosts and, at 128, learns degraded-host avoidance
completely while load-balancing less tightly. A DQN cross-check reproduces the
same concentration under the same remedy, which points at the problem
formulation rather than at one algorithm.

\item Release the harness, trained models, raw per-call records and the saved
output of every run, including the configurations that produce the measurement
artifacts above, so that each can be reproduced and checked.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Elastic and Fault-Tolerant State Management}

NF state management for elastic scaling follows two strategies. State migration
moves NF state between instances. Split/Merge~\cite{rajagopalan2013split} splits state
into per-flow and global parts; global updates merge periodically and stay eventually
consistent, but in-flight traffic can be dropped during migration. OpenNF~\cite{gember2014opennf}
offers move, copy, and share operations for different consistency levels. Both systems need a central controller to coordinate migration. 

Migration avoidance instead decouples state from processing. StatelessNF~\cite{kablan2017stateless,szalay2019industrial} keeps state in a remote data store, adding per-packet latency and a single point of failure. S6~\cite{woo2018elastic} spreads state across a Chord-like key/object layer~\cite{stoica2003chord}, reducing, but not eliminating, migration through caching and
replication.

NF fault tolerance instead relies on replication and checkpointing. Pico Replication~\cite{rajagopalan2013pico} checkpoints frequently~\cite{plank1994libckpt} for loss-free recovery, but does not handle in-flight packets arriving during a checkpoint. FTMB~\cite{sherry2015rollback} checkpoints infrequently and logs and replays packets instead, favoring throughput over checkpoint frequency. REINFORCE~\cite{kulkarni2018reinforce} combines both: packets are buffered at a predecessor node and replayed to a backup on failure, with lazy
checkpointing except for non-deterministic packets~\cite{cachin2016non}, which force a
strict checkpoint. All three assume a backup already exists for each primary; none
describe how it is chosen.

DEFT~\cite{shahriyar2023deft} spans both strands: one distributed 2PC protocol handles
elastic scaling and fault tolerance together, with no central controller
(unlike~\cite{gember2014opennf,rajagopalan2013split}). But it inherits the fault-tolerant systems' gap: backup assignment is a bookkeeping step in its Failure Detection Unit, not a studied decision.

\textbf{The separation the table makes.} Prior work specifies \emph{how} state
reaches another host in considerable detail and \emph{which} host it reaches
hardly at all. The storage systems in the last two rows are the exception, and
are why we adopt weighted hashing as a baseline family (\S\ref{sec:hrw}): they
show that quality-aware target selection is well understood where replica
placement is the subject, and simply has not been carried across to NF state
replication.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for NFV Resource Management}

A separate line of work applies reinforcement learning to NFV resource management,
almost entirely for primary placement, scaling, or embedding, not backup/replica
selection. Cand-RL~\cite{santos2022reinforcement} uses PPO for availability-aware Service
Function Chain (SFC) placement. DDQN-VNFPA~\cite{pei2019optimal} uses Double Deep
Q-Networks for VNF placement. Two recent surveys, on RL for virtual network
embedding~\cite{lim2023reinforcement} and on RL for network slice scaling and
placement~\cite{saha2023deep}, both catalogue placement, scaling, and embedding as RL's
NFV sub-problems; backup/replica selection for fault tolerance appears in neither.

\subsection{Replica or Backup Selection}
\label{sec:related-backup}

Where the elastic and fault-tolerant state-management systems above specify how
state is replicated or migrated, they take the choice of backup host as given, left
to an implicit default rather than chosen from the candidate hosts' resource state. The few
efforts that come near replica-like selection approach it from a different angle. Kibalya et
al.~\cite{kibalya2021reinforcement} place stateful VNFs, but the state guiding that
placement is a primary's own execution state, not a fault-tolerance relationship between a
primary and a distinct backup. Avgeris et al.~\cite{avgeris2023reinforcement}, the closest in
spirit, reposition VNFs once a failure or contention event has already been detected,
a reactive remedy applied after the fact, whereas we choose a secondary proactively, as
replication is being established.

The idea that replica placement deserves algorithmic attention in its own right is, by
contrast, long established outside NFV, in distributed storage. CRUSH~\cite{weil2006crush}
computes failure-domain and load-aware placement without a central lookup table, and
Dynamo~\cite{decandia2007dynamo} spreads replicas via consistent hashing with
capacity-aware preference lists; both make ``which node holds the copy'' a first-class,
criteria-driven decision. Their underlying mechanism, decentralized weighted hashing
(rendezvous/HRW and consistent hashing), carries over directly to NF backups, which is
why we adopt it as a baseline family (\S\ref{sec:hrw}). We find that such stateless schemes trail a lightly-coordinated selector on load-balancing. To our knowledge, no prior work brings this first-class treatment to \emph{proactive backup-NF selection} for stateful NFV fault tolerance, the gap this paper addresses.

\section{Backup-Selection Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

Let $P$ be the set of primary NFs and $H$ the set of all hosts (a superset of $P$; any
host not currently a primary for a given flow, is a feasible backup for that primary, including hosts that are primaries for other flows). For each primary $p \in P$ we must choose a backup
$b \in H \setminus \{p\}$. Each candidate host $h$ has a dynamic resource state: health
$\mathrm{health}(h) \in [0,1]$, remaining capacity $\mathrm{cap}(h)$ out of
$\mathrm{cap}_{\max}(h)$, CPU and storage availability, and a topology-derived hop-count
and latency to $p$. Concretely, per host and per random seed these are drawn independently
as $\mathrm{cap}_{\max} \sim \mathcal{U}\{50,\dots,100\}$ (integer), with $\mathrm{cap}$
initialised to $\mathrm{cap}_{\max}$; $\mathrm{health} \sim \mathcal{U}[0.5,1.0]$; and CPU
and storage availability each $\sim \mathcal{U}[0.4,1.0]$; latency is
$1.5\,\text{ms} \times \mathrm{hops}$. Degraded hosts in the heterogeneous scenarios
(\S\ref{sec:methodology}) are constructed under one of two models
(\S\ref{sec:weights}). The \emph{binary} model, used for the latency experiments and for
all previously published results, pins $\mathrm{health}=0.05$, $\mathrm{cap}=2$,
$\mathrm{cap}_{\max}=60$ (capacity ratio $\approx 0.033$). The \emph{realistic} model
draws a continuous severity and one of five failure kinds per host, leaves the untouched
features at their healthy draw, and gives healthy hosts a baseline utilisation so that
remaining capacity is a continuous measurement rather than a flag. Infrastructure hosts (client,
stamper) are never sampled as backups.

\textbf{From synthetic features to real telemetry.} These four features are deliberately
chosen to correspond to signals a production NFV orchestrator already exposes, so the
abstraction is a stand-in for measurable metrics rather than an idealization unavailable in
practice. $\mathrm{health}$ maps to a composite liveness/health score: failure-detector
confidence, recent error/drop rate, or hardware SMART/thermal indicators, as surfaced by an
ETSI NFV-MANO VNF health indication or a Kubernetes readiness/liveness probe. The capacity
ratio $\mathrm{cap}/\mathrm{cap}_{\max}$ maps to headroom in the NF's dominant scaling
resource: free flow-table or connection-tracking entries, session/NAT-binding slots, or
admission-control headroom against a provisioned limit. CPU and storage availability map to
standard hypervisor/OS utilization ($1-\text{util}$, free state-store fraction) exported by
cAdvisor, node-exporter, or libvirt; hop-count and latency map to the SDN controller's
topology view and active RTT probing. Validating the selection against such real workload
telemetry, rather than the synthetic draws used here, remains future work
(\S\ref{sec:threats}).

\subsection{Baselines}

\textbf{Static (DEFT's shipped mapping).} The hand-assigned mapping DEFT ships with for
its 9-host reference topology, regenerable on demand for direct comparison.

\textbf{Concentrated.} Every primary assigned to the same single feasible backup: a
scale-independent, maximally naive worst case, generalizing DEFT's own static mapping's
tendency to over-concentrate load onto one host.

\textbf{Random.} A uniformly random feasible backup per primary, seeded for
reproducibility: a sanity floor any non-random method should beat.

\textbf{Round-robin.} Cycles through the sorted list of all feasible hosts, skipping a
candidate only when it equals the primary itself. Load-balances blindly, with zero
awareness of host health, capacity, or latency. We also evaluate a \emph{randomized-offset}
variant that begins the cycle at a random position per run, so its degraded-host exposure
reflects the degraded fraction rather than an artifact of the fixed host ordering
(\S\ref{sec:hrw}).

\textbf{Rendezvous hashing (HRW) and consistent hashing.} \emph{Plain HRW} assigns each
primary to the feasible backup with the highest deterministic hash weight
$\mathrm{hash}(p,c)$: fully decentralized (no coordinator, no cross-primary state) and
load-spreading in expectation, but health/capacity-unaware, the NFV analog of a
consistent-hashing replica placement. \emph{Weighted HRW} weights the hash by
$\mathrm{health}(c)\cdot\mathrm{cap}(c)/\mathrm{cap}_{\max}(c)$ (in the spirit of
CRUSH/Dynamo node weights), making it quality-aware while remaining decentralized.
\emph{Weighted consistent hashing} is a second decentralized quality-weighted point: a
Dynamo-style hash ring with capacity/health-proportional virtual nodes (a host whose weight
rounds to zero, e.g.\ a degraded host, places none and is never selected). Unlike the
heuristic, none of these apply an inter-primary load penalty, so they spread only
statistically.

\subsection{Optimal: Capacity-Constrained Hungarian Assignment}
\label{sec:optimal}

To obtain an upper bound the heuristic and RL policy can be judged against, we solve the
assignment problem exactly, using the same multi-criteria score $\mathrm{score}(p,c)$
defined in the next subsection (Eq.~\eqref{eq:score}: a weighted combination of health,
remaining capacity, latency, and hop-count). Each feasible backup $h$ is expanded into
$k = \lceil |P| / |H_{\mathrm{feas}}| \rceil$ capacity-constrained columns, where
$H_{\mathrm{feas}}$ is the set of backup-eligible hosts (all hosts except the client and
stamper); the cost-matrix entry for primary $p$ and column $j$ (backup $h$) is
$-\mathrm{score}(p,h)$, or a large fixed penalty ($10^6$) if $h$ is infeasible for $p$
(including $h=p$). Because backup-eligible hosts always outnumber primaries in our
topologies, $k=1$ at every scale evaluated here ($\lceil 5/7 \rceil$, $\lceil 10/16 \rceil$,
$\lceil 20/32 \rceil$, $\lceil 80/128 \rceil$ for the 9/16/32/128-host topologies, all
equal to $1$), so the solver assigns each primary to a distinct backup and the optimum
attains a uniform maximum load of one; the $k>1$ machinery is retained for denser regimes
in which primaries would outnumber feasible backups. We solve this exactly via the
Hungarian algorithm~\cite{kuhn1955hungarian}
(\texttt{scipy.optimize.linear\_sum\_assignment}~\cite{virtanen2020scipy}), yielding a
globally optimal assignment under the capacity constraint, something no greedy or online
method can guarantee in general. Solving it requires the complete cost matrix over all
primaries and candidate backups at once, which in turn requires a party with a global view
to gather that state and run the solver before any assignment is made. This centralized
computation is in tension with DEFT's distributed, controller-free design
(\S\ref{sec:intro}), so we use the Hungarian assignment strictly as an upper-bound
benchmark, not as a deployment candidate.

\subsection{Heuristic: Multi-Criteria Greedy Scoring}
\label{sec:heuristic}

For each primary (processed in a fixed order), the heuristic scores every feasible
candidate $c$ as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score}
\begin{split}
\mathrm{score}(p, c) ={}& w_h \cdot \mathrm{health}(c) + w_k \cdot \frac{\mathrm{cap}(c)}{\mathrm{cap}_{\max}(c)} \\
&- w_\ell \cdot \hat\ell(p,c) - w_d \cdot \hat{d}(p,c)
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\hat\ell$ and $\hat{d}$ are the min-max-normalized latency and hop-count from $p$
to $c$ among $p$'s candidates, and default weights
$(w_h, w_k, w_\ell, w_d) = (0.35, 0.35, 0.20, 0.10)$ favor health and capacity, which are
what actually differentiate candidates once a topology has heterogeneous backup quality.
The greedy assignment picks $\arg\max_c \mathrm{score}(p,c)$ for each primary in turn,
then applies a load penalty to the chosen host ($\mathrm{cap} \mathrel{-}= 20$;
$\mathrm{health} \mathrel{*}= 0.95$) before moving to the next primary. This penalty is
the mechanism that spreads load across the backup pool without any learning component.

\subsection{RL: PPO with Invalid-Action Masking}

We train a Proximal Policy Optimization~\cite{schulman2017proximal} agent
(\texttt{stable-baselines3}~\cite{raffin2021stable} over a
\texttt{gymnasium}~\cite{towers2024gymnasium} environment) on the same scoring objective
as the heuristic. One episode assigns a backup to every primary in sequence, under one
random resource realization; the observation is each host's
$[\mathrm{health}, \mathrm{cap}/\mathrm{cap}_{\max}, \mathrm{cpu}, \mathrm{storage}]$
concatenated with a one-hot encoding of the currently-active primary, and the action
space is $\mathrm{Discrete}(|H|)$ (observation dimension $4|H|+|H|$). The reward is
$\mathrm{score}(p,c)$ for a feasible choice, minus an overload penalty of $-2$ for each
primary already assigned to that backup (i.e.\ $-2(\ell-1)$ when the choice makes host $c$
the $\ell$-th primary's backup), and a large fixed penalty ($-10$) for an infeasible
choice, so invalid actions are discouraged \emph{during training}, not merely corrected at
inference time as in a naive post-hoc-masking design. A fresh random resource realization
is drawn every episode so the learned policy generalizes across resource realizations
rather than memorizing one instance. At inference, the deterministic policy is rolled out
once per primary, falling back to the best-scoring feasible candidate in the rare case the policy still selects an infeasible action.

Table~\ref{tab:ppohparams} lists the full per-model training configuration; all other PPO
settings are \texttt{stable-baselines3} defaults (learning rate $3\times10^{-4}$,
$\gamma=0.99$, GAE $\lambda=0.95$, clip range $0.2$, 10 optimization epochs per update).
Each model is trained for its own host count, so a differently-sized topology requires a
freshly trained model (\S\ref{sec:threats}); the two 128-host variants are the collapsed
default-hyperparameter baseline and the reported fix of \S\ref{sec:rl128}, both released as
checkpoints. The 128-host \emph{intermediate} model (which only partially improved the
spread) is listed for completeness but not shipped. \S\ref{sec:dqn} additionally trains a
DQN agent on this same environment and reward, as a cross-algorithm check on the
load-balancing behavior PPO exhibits at large host counts.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{Testbed: RPC-Isolation, Not Physical Mininet}

We measure backup-selection quality by driving the \emph{real, unmodified} \texttt{Backup}
and \texttt{Primary} 2PC classes from DEFT's implementation directly over XML-RPC, with
each backup host running as its own OS process on localhost. This is a deliberate
departure from a physical Mininet network path, which we attempted first: the
development environment's kernel lacked the \texttt{openvswitch} kernel module and the
\texttt{htb} traffic-control queueing discipline, no SDN controller was included in the
original repository, and, decisively, \texttt{TCLink} bandwidth throttling did not
engage on the available kernel, so there was no real resource constraint for contention
to manifest against over the physical network path. The RPC-isolation testbed, by
directly exercising the actual backup RPC server under controlled concurrent load, gives
a more direct and reproducible measurement of the contention mechanism in question, at
the cost of not validating full end-to-end network behavior (switching, real link
bandwidth, packet loss); see \S\ref{sec:threats}.

Each primary fires a \emph{burst} of simultaneously in-flight RPC calls rather than one sequential call-then-wait stream, matching how concurrent primaries would load a shared
backup in production. Three implementation pitfalls were identified and corrected during
harness development: (1) running backup servers as threads within one process shares a
GIL, confounding the very contention effect being measured, so servers now run as
separate OS processes; (2) each burst worker holds one \texttt{ServerProxy} for all of its calls, with
retry-with-jittered-backoff on transient connection resets. We initially described this
as reusing one TCP connection per worker; it does not. DEFT's \texttt{Backup} inherits
\texttt{SimpleXMLRPCServer}'s default \texttt{protocol\_version} of HTTP/1.0, so the
server closes the connection after every response and the proxy silently reconnects: we
measure twenty calls through a single proxy producing twenty \texttt{accept()}s
server-side. Every replication call therefore performs a fresh TCP handshake against a
five-deep accept backlog, which is the direct cause of the tail behavior analysed in
\S\ref{sec:p99}; and (3) an early
version built a single host-resource context per seed and passed that same object to every
method, but the heuristic and RL selectors apply an in-place load-decay penalty to each
host they pick, so any method evaluated after them scored against an already-depleted
context and its measured mapping depended on execution order, so each method now receives
its own copy of the context.

A fourth issue surfaced only at the largest scale. \texttt{xmlrpc.client} applies no
socket timeout, so a connection the server has accepted but is not reading stalls the
client indefinitely. Because the backup is single-threaded, one such connection blocks
its accept loop and every other client queues behind it: at 128 hosts with 80 primaries
on one backup we observed a run frozen for 80\,s with the server's CPU time, its
established-connection count, and its SYN-SENT backlog all constant, making no forward
progress at all. We therefore bound each call with a 20\,s client-side socket timeout,
well above the largest genuine service time observed anywhere in these experiments
(7.2\,s), so it breaks the deadlock without censoring real slow calls. As with the
retry budget, this changes the benchmark client, not the backup server under test.

\subsection{The Second Transport}

Alongside DEFT's shipped XML-RPC path we implement the same replication call over
gRPC: a service exposing exactly the method DEFT's backup exposes
(\texttt{update\_state(state)} returning an acknowledgement), one persistent
HTTP/2 channel per primary rather than a connection per call, and a server thread
pool in place of a single accept loop. Message payloads carry the same flow-keyed
state. The selection methods, scoring function, topologies, degraded-host
injection, workload, retry policy and statistical analysis are identical across the
two arms, so the transport is the only thing that varies.

Two details keep the comparison honest. First, the gRPC server's worker-pool size
is an explicit parameter rather than an inherited default, so the transport change
can be separated from the concurrency change; setting it to one reproduces the
XML-RPC backup's strict serialization over HTTP/2. Second, gRPC reports channel
readiness exactly, so backup startup is synchronised rather than waited out with a
fixed sleep, removing a source of spurious early-call errors present in the
XML-RPC arm.

\subsection{Scenarios}

\textbf{Homogeneous (9 hosts).} DEFT's real reference topology, 5 primaries, synthetic
resource values drawn independently per random seed with no engineered heterogeneity.

\textbf{Scaled heterogeneous (16, 32, 128 hosts).} Synthetic star topologies at
increasing scale (10/20/80 primaries; 6/12/48 backup-eligible hosts), with
$\lfloor|H \setminus P| / 3\rfloor$ of the backup-eligible hosts deliberately degraded
(health $=0.05$, capacity ratio $\approx 0.03$, plus an artificial +50\,ms per-call
server-side delay simulating a genuinely overloaded machine). This isolates whether
feature-aware methods avoid known-bad hosts, independent of load-balancing alone, and
whether the finding holds as the candidate pool grows by roughly an order of magnitude.

\subsection{Statistical Methodology}
\label{sec:stats}

We draw multiple independent topology/resource realizations (context seeds) rather than
repeating trials of one fixed instance, since repeated trials of a single instance only
capture RPC-timing noise, not whether a result generalizes across problem instances.

\textbf{The unit of analysis is a run, not a call.} One \emph{run} is one
(method, context seed, trial) execution. The individual RPC calls within a run are not
independent observations: a burst's calls share a TCP connection sequence, a
single-threaded backup server process, an OS scheduler, and one resource realization.
Treating each call as a sample inflates $n$ into the tens of thousands and drives
$p$-values to zero without adding evidence. The effect is easy to quantify: on synthetic
data built from two methods with \emph{no} true difference and correlated calls within
each run, a Mann-Whitney $U$ test pooled over 15{,}000 calls returns
$p = 1.1\times10^{-44}$, while the run-level test on the same data correctly returns
$p = 0.76$. We therefore reduce each run to per-run statistics (mean, median, p95
service latency, and failure rate over that run's own calls) and analyse those, giving
$n = 15$ at every scale reported here.

\textbf{Comparisons are paired.} Every method is executed under each (context seed,
trial) condition, so methods are compared by a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on
the paired per-run differences, with the matched-pairs rank-biserial correlation
$r = (T^{+}-T^{-})/(T^{+}+T^{-})$ as effect size; $r = \pm 1$ means every run agrees on
the direction. Pairing matters here because the between-run variance is large and
common-mode (\S\ref{sec:p99}), and pairing cancels it exactly. We apply
Holm-Bonferroni correction across the full family of pairwise comparisons at each scale,
and report cluster-bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (5000 resamples) that resample
whole runs rather than individual calls. Per-trial method execution order is randomized
so no method is systematically favored by background system load.

\textbf{Failed calls are censored.} A call that exhausts its retry budget contributes to
the reported failure rate and to nothing else. Imputing a sentinel latency for it, as our
earlier harness did, contaminates every percentile computed from the sample.

\textbf{Reporting mean and p95, not p99.} \S\ref{sec:p99} shows that p99 in this
testbed measures the kernel's TCP SYN-retransmission timer rather than replication
latency. We therefore report mean and p95 throughout, and use p99 only where the point
is the tail pathology itself.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Transport as a Controlled Variable}
\label{sec:p99}

We evaluate every method over two transports. The first is DEFT's shipped path:
its \texttt{Backup} is a \texttt{SimpleXMLRPCServer}, which is single-threaded,
speaks HTTP/1.0, and listens with a backlog of five. The second is gRPC over
HTTP/2, with one persistent channel per primary and a server thread pool, which
is representative of how production NFV systems carry state replication. The two
expose the same method, perform the same work per call, and are driven by the
same selection methods, topologies, workloads and statistics; only the delivery
mechanism differs. The transport is therefore a controlled variable, and a
finding that holds under both is a property of backup placement rather than of
one RPC stack.

This matters because the shipped transport distorts the measurement in a specific
and quantifiable way.

\textbf{On XML-RPC the latency tail is a kernel timer, not queueing delay.}
HTTP/1.0 means the server closes the connection after each response, so every
replication call performs a fresh TCP handshake. We verified this directly:
twenty calls issued through a single client proxy produce twenty server-side
\texttt{accept()}s. Under burst load the five-deep backlog overflows, the SYN is
dropped, and the client waits out the kernel's retransmission timer. The
resulting distribution is sharply bimodal with an empty gap between the modes:
at 16 hosts, $82.3\%$ of calls complete under $100$\,ms and $3.2\%$ beyond
$900$\,ms, while the $500$--$900$\,ms band holds $0.042\%$.

\noindent
Queueing produces a smooth tail; this does not. The 900--1100\,ms calls sit at
$1052$--$1059$\,ms with a standard deviation of 24--29\,ms at every scale, which
is Linux's initial SYN-retransmission timeout, with the next retry contributing
the $\approx$3\,s cluster. Consequently p99 on this transport is a threshold
statistic rather than a latency measurement: since 1--2\% of calls land on the
timer, it reads either tens of milliseconds or $\approx$1050\,ms depending only
on whether a run's SYN-drop rate crosses one percent.

\textbf{Under gRPC the pile-up disappears.} Figure~\ref{fig:tailtransport} plots
the complementary CDF of per-call service time for both arms at 16 hosts
(180{,}000 and 300{,}000 calls of the same workload). The XML-RPC curve is a
staircase: it runs flat and then steps down at each timer quantum, with
\textbf{3.20\%} of calls landing beyond 900\,ms and a maximum of 22{,}773\,ms.
The gRPC curve decays smoothly, with \textbf{0.013\%} beyond 900\,ms, a $240\times$
difference in tail mass, and a maximum of 1139\,ms. Percentiles follow: p95
101.9 vs 360.6\,ms, p99 184.9 vs 1175.8\,ms, p99.9 277.9 vs 7261.6\,ms.

The 500--900\,ms band itself is sparsely occupied on \emph{both} arms (0.042\% and
0.057\%), which is the point: on the gRPC arm that sparsity is simply the thin
end of a decaying distribution, whereas on the XML-RPC arm it is a gap with 3.2\%
of the mass piled immediately beyond it. A queueing tail does not have that shape.
p99 is a usable metric on the gRPC arm, and we report it there.

\subsection{128 Hosts on One Machine: Structural Results}
\label{sec:rl128}

At 128 hosts (80 primaries) a single machine cannot produce a trustworthy latency
measurement: 800 client threads and some 600 server threads contend on eight
cores, and two independent executions of the identical gRPC configuration differ
by $4.8\times$ on \texttt{optimal} and $6.1\times$ on PPO. We therefore report no
latency from this configuration; \S\ref{sec:testbed} supplies that measurement on
bare metal. One effect survives the noise --- \texttt{concentrated} is worse than
every method with $r = 1.000$ on both transports and in both executions, and is
the only configuration where client retries contribute measurably at all
($3.53\%$ of calls, against $0.00\%$ everywhere else).

What remains valid here is the \emph{structure} of the produced mapping, which is
computed from the assignment and does not depend on timing
(Table~\ref{tab:scaled128}). The deterministic heuristic spreads 80 primaries
across 74--76 distinct backups while the learned policies use 24--30, and
\S\ref{sec:testbed} confirms this reading on hardware.

\textbf{Why the learned policies concentrate.} PPO at this scale is sensitive to
exploration and capacity in a way it is not at 32 hosts. With
\texttt{stable-baselines3}'s default entropy coefficient of zero and a $[64,64]$
network, the policy converged to 1--5 distinct backups of 80: nothing discourages
locking onto one action that looks adequate on average, and a small network has
limited capacity to condition on which of 80 one-hot primaries is active.
Retraining with entropy $0.02$, a $[256,256]$ network and 1M timesteps widened
the spread substantially; both checkpoints are released.

\textbf{What the anti-concentration term is worth.} The reward already penalizes
concentration directly, by $-2(\ell-1)$ for making a host the $\ell$-th primary's
backup, so the question is whether that strength suffices.
Table~\ref{tab:rlablation} sweeps it over two training seeds per setting. The
term delivers a real benefit whose size depends sharply on scale: at 32 hosts
removing it costs about three distinct backups and restoring even a small penalty
recovers most of the benefit, so the published strength of $2$ sits on a flat part
of the curve; at 128 hosts it is not optional, with the policy collapsing to $3.2$
distinct backups at a peak load of $55$ without it against $13.7$ with it.

The response is also \emph{non-monotone}, and this replicates across both training
seeds: a penalty of $10$ is far worse than the published $2$ ($5.9$ distinct
backups against $13.7$) and barely better than none. A plausible mechanism is that
with 80 primaries sharing the pool some repetition is unavoidable, so a large
penalty accumulates negative reward the agent cannot avoid and destabilizes value
estimation. What the term does not do at either scale is close the gap to the
untrained heuristic, which reaches 74--76 of 80 at no training cost. The agent is
credited for concentration at the step where it occurs while the consequences
accrue over the rest of the episode; scoring the assignment as a whole is the more
promising direction (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{Cross-Algorithm Check: DQN}
\label{sec:dqn}

The load-balancing gap of \S\ref{sec:rl128} is measured on one algorithm, which
leaves open whether it reflects PPO or the problem formulation. We repeat the
evaluation with Deep Q-Networks~\cite{mnih2015human}, a deliberately different
design point --- off-policy with a replay buffer, value-based rather than
actor-critic, exploring by $\epsilon$-greedy decay rather than an entropy bonus
--- through the same environment, reward and inference path, so only the learner
differs. Both are scored \emph{structurally} (Table~\ref{tab:ppodqn}): these are
properties of the produced mapping rather than of RPC timing, which makes them
hardware-independent, and Table~\ref{tab:backupload} has already established backup
load as what drives latency under contention.

The two algorithms agree where the problem is easy and diverge where it is hard.
Through 32 hosts both spread well and avoid degraded hosts. At 128 hosts DQN
collapses further than PPO ever did, onto 1--3 of 80 backups with a peak load of
45--80 and 172 of 400 assignments landing on degraded hosts --- the latter a
failure PPO does not share. Widening exploration lifts DQN at 32 hosts from 2--4
distinct backups to 16--19, the same remedy and the same partial success as PPO's
entropy and capacity increase, and at 128 hosts it collapses regardless.

That two algorithms with different exploration mechanisms, update rules and
objectives fail in the same way under the same remedy points at the formulation
rather than at either learner. The reward credits a policy for concentration at
the step where it occurs while the consequences accrue over the remainder of the
episode, and scoring the assignment as a whole rather than one primary at a time
is the more promising direction (\S\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{Degradation Realism, Weight Sensitivity, and What the Scoring Terms Buy}
\label{sec:weights}

\S\ref{sec:methods} fixes the heuristic's scoring weights at
$(w_h, w_k, w_\ell, w_d) = (0.35, 0.35, 0.20, 0.10)$ and its load-decay mechanics
($\mathrm{cap} \mathrel{-}= 20$; $\mathrm{health} \mathrel{*}= 0.95$) by intuition. To
test whether those are principled, we sweep 287 weight configurations (286 points on a
0.1-step grid over the 4-simplex, plus the published default, which does not fall on that
grid) across four topologies with five context-seed draws each. The sweep is
\emph{structural}: it evaluates the produced mapping directly, with no RPC calls, so it is
exact and reproducible.

\textbf{How degraded hosts are constructed determines what the sweep can tell us.} Our
first model marked a host degraded by pinning it to one extreme point: health $0.05$
against a healthy draw of $U[0.5, 1.0]$, and a capacity ratio of $0.033$ against a healthy
ratio of $1.0$. Degraded hosts were thus separated from healthy ones by an order of
magnitude on \emph{both} scoring features at once, and every degraded host was identical.
Under that model all 287 configurations at all four topologies achieved 0\% degraded-host
usage, including a configuration placing 100\% of its weight on hop-count and therefore
unable to see host quality at all. A result that holds even for a scoring function with no
discriminative power is a property of the benchmark, not of the method, so we do not claim
it.

We therefore introduce a second, harder model. Severity is drawn continuously rather than
pinned, so some hosts are genuinely marginal; degradation has five kinds
(CPU starvation, failing health, capacity exhaustion, storage pressure, and link
congestion), each driving a different subset of the features, so a selector reading only
one signal no longer catches everything; degradation is partial, so a host can be slow but
healthy or healthy but nearly full; and healthy hosts carry a baseline utilisation, so
remaining capacity is a continuous measurement rather than a flag that only degraded hosts
trip. The last point also addresses a formulation gap: previously every non-degraded host
presented as completely idle regardless of how many primaries it already backed.

\textbf{Under realistic degradation the sweep discriminates sharply, and one failure mode
defeats the published scoring function.} At 32 and 128 hosts, 80 and 111 of 286
configurations route at least one primary to a degraded host. The heuristic at its default
weights went from $0.0$ to $0.8$ degraded assignments per run at 32 hosts. The cause is
specific: the \texttt{link\_congested} kind adds 20\,ms to every call while reading
\emph{above average} on health, capacity, CPU and storage, because its degradation lives in
the network path rather than in any resource gauge. The two features that could reveal it,
latency and hop-count, are derived from the topology graph, and in a uniform-hop star every
backup-eligible host is exactly two hops from every primary, so both normalize to zero and
carry no signal whatsoever.

\textbf{Scoring against measured RTT restores the guarantee.} A production orchestrator
does not have to infer path latency from hop-count; it has active RTT probing, which is
precisely the telemetry \S\ref{sec:methods} maps the latency feature onto. Replacing the
topology-derived latency term with measured round-trip time, which includes whatever delay
the candidate is currently adding, returns degraded-host assignments to $0.0$ at 32 hosts
and $0.1$ at 128. The single residual case is a host at severity $0.12$ adding $11.5$\,ms,
which is genuinely marginal rather than a miss.

\textbf{This inverts which scoring terms matter.} Table~\ref{tab:terms} reports, for each
term, how often a configuration that zeroes it routes a primary to a degraded host. The
latency term, evaluated against measured RTT, is what provides degraded-host avoidance:
zeroing it causes 55 of 66 configurations to fail at both scales. Zeroing the health term
causes \emph{none} to fail. Under the binary model this question had no answer at all,
since every configuration passed. The published default survives with zero degraded
assignments at every scale, but now for an identifiable reason, namely its non-zero latency
weight, rather than because every configuration passes.

One caveat bounds this. Every degradation kind in our model carries some latency
signature, which is realistic (a degraded host does serve more slowly) but makes measured
RTT close to a sufficient statistic in this benchmark. A failure mode with no latency
signature, such as a host that is currently fast but about to fail, would defeat it and
would need the health signal instead. The defensible claim is therefore not that latency
is the only term worth having, but that a scoring function restricted to static resource
gauges is blind to network-path degradation, and that the remedy is measured rather than
inferred path latency.

\textbf{Load-balancing quality is governed by the capacity decay term.} Separately from
host quality, maximum per-backup load ranges across the sweep from perfect (one
primary per backup) to severe (79 of 80 primaries sharing one backup at 128 hosts, when
capacity and health weights are both zero). The capacity term, realised as the flat $-20$
decrement applied after each assignment, is the dominant lever: even a small non-zero
capacity weight reaches near-optimal balance at every scale. The health decay
($\times 0.95$) is a weaker lever toward the same end. Sweeping both decay constants (the
decrement over $\{5,10,20,40,80\}$ and the health factor over
$\{0.80,0.90,0.95,0.99,1.00\}$, 100 cells) shows load-balancing to be a smooth, monotone
function of both: holding the health decay at $0.95$, raising the decrement through
$5, 10, 20, 40, 80$ lowers mean maximum per-backup load through
$2.60, 2.00, 2.00, 1.00, 1.00$. The published decrement of 20 is thus a deliberately
conservative operating point within one unit of the perfect-spread optimum, leaving a
small bounded amount of load-balancing unclaimed rather than spreading maximally
aggressively, which on a pool with finer quality variation could trade host quality for
balance.

\subsection{Decentralized Baselines, a Fairer Round-Robin, and Topologies}
\label{sec:hrw}

We broaden the baseline set along three axes the load-balancing and
distributed-storage literature would expect, scored structurally (degraded-host
hits and maximum per-backup load, 5-seed average) in the heterogeneous scenarios
(Table~\ref{tab:hrw}).

\textbf{Decentralized hash placement.} We add rendezvous hashing
(\texttt{hrw}), health/capacity-weighted rendezvous hashing, and Dynamo-style
weighted consistent hashing, in the style of
CRUSH~\cite{weil2006crush} and Dynamo~\cite{decandia2007dynamo}. Plain HRW is
quality-unaware and its degraded-host exposure is correspondingly uncontrolled.
Both weighted variants avoid degraded hosts entirely at every scale, which
establishes that \emph{quality-awareness, not centralization, is what buys
degraded-host avoidance}. They are, however, coordination-free --- each primary
hashes independently with no load penalty propagated --- so they spread load only
statistically: mean peak per-backup load is $3.0/3.6/2.2$ and $2.8/3.0/3.2$ at
16/32/128 hosts, against the greedy heuristic's $1.4/2.0/2.0$ and the exact
assignment's $1.0$. A single coordinated greedy pass load-balances materially
tighter than either weighted-hash family at equal degraded-host avoidance.

\textbf{A fairer round-robin.} Deterministic round-robin's degraded-host exposure
depends on the host-name sort order, an artifact we examine directly in
\S\ref{sec:testbed}. A randomized-offset variant removes it: with a random
starting offset it meets degraded hosts at \emph{every} scale, in proportion to
how many there are ($1.2/2.6/10.8$ primaries at 16/32/128). Any quality-blind
load-spreader is exposed in proportion to the degraded fraction; the feature-aware
methods avoid them by construction.

\textbf{Topology diversity.} Repeating the comparison on multi-hub topologies,
where host-to-host hop count and latency vary, leaves the structural outcome
unchanged: the heuristic still avoids degraded hosts entirely at a peak load the
hash families do not match. Degraded-host avoidance and load balancing are driven
by host quality rather than graph shape, so the findings do not depend on the
single-hub star.

Elastic scaling requires the selection itself to be fast: a method that matches
the optimal bound but takes seconds to compute is a worse deployment story than
one that does not. Table~\ref{tab:overhead} reports mean wall-clock time per full

log--log axes in Fig.~\ref{fig:overhead}.

Even the slowest method computes a 128-host mapping in about $60$\,ms (PPO,

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\textbf{Synthetic resource values (the primary external-validity gap).} Health,
capacity, CPU and storage availability are drawn from the configured ranges of
\S\ref{sec:methods} rather than measured from live telemetry, so absolute
magnitudes are illustrative rather than calibrated. \S\ref{sec:methods} maps each
feature onto the orchestrator signal it stands in for, and \S\ref{sec:weights}
shows the mapping matters: a host degraded only in its network path defeats a
scoring function built on static gauges until latency is measured rather than
derived. Validating against live telemetry is the most important remaining work,
and limits the ecological validity of the absolute, though not the relative,
results.

\textbf{RPC-isolation testbed, not a packet-level data path.} We drive DEFT's
real backup server directly rather than over an emulated network, after the
available Mininet environment could not impose a genuine bandwidth constraint.
This gives a direct, reproducible measurement of the contention mechanism at the
cost of not exercising DEFT's packet-level, controller-integrated path. A
packet-level evaluation over a working SDN fabric, against which the RPC-level
failure rate could be cross-checked with DEFT's native packet-drop metric,
remains outstanding; we treat this study as isolating the selection mechanism
rather than certifying end-to-end performance.

\textbf{Two transports, not every transport.} Evaluating over both XML-RPC and
gRPC establishes that the findings are not an artifact of one RPC stack, and
\S\ref{sec:testbed} varies the accept queue directly. Kernel-bypass paths (DPDK,
RDMA) remove the kernel networking stack entirely and would queue differently
again. The ordering of methods is driven by how many primaries share a backup,
which we expect to be robust; absolute magnitudes are specific to the transports
measured.

\textbf{What the bare-metal run revised, and what the 9-host scale inherits.}
\S\ref{sec:testbed} changed more than it confirmed: most of the apparent cost of
concentrating backups was the shipped accept queue rather than load on the
backup, and with homogeneous hosts no method separates from random. The 16- and
32-host scenarios have been re-run on that hardware and the ordering holds. The
9-host scenario has not, so its magnitudes are measured over the shipped backlog,
whose bias at small scale runs toward understating rather than inflating the gap
between methods (\S\ref{sec:testbed}).

\textbf{Absolute latencies are not reproducible across executions.}
Ephemeral-port TIME\_WAIT pressure shifts the mean of every method by of order
$17$\,ms between otherwise identical single-machine executions
(\S\ref{sec:p99}). The drift is common-mode, so the paired design cancels it, but
it sets a resolution floor of roughly $10$\,ms on that harness, below which we
make no claim. Four candidate effects of that size reached significance in one
execution and vanished on replication, which is why every scale is run twice.

\textbf{Deterministic round-robin's exposure is a function of naming.} Its
degraded-host exposure depends on how the host-name sort aligns with the degraded
set, which \S\ref{sec:testbed} examines directly and \S\ref{sec:hrw} removes with
a randomized-offset variant. This does not affect the load-bearing claim, that
feature-aware methods avoid degraded hosts by construction at every scale, which
no round-robin variant offers.

\textbf{RL configuration scales with the action space.} A policy's observation
and action space is fixed to a host count, so each topology is served by its own
trained model, and exploration and capacity budgets must grow with the action
space (\S\ref{sec:rl128}). Reported RL numbers describe the released checkpoints;
retraining under a different framework version yields a different but
qualitatively similar policy.

\textbf{Coverage is a sweep, not a continuum.} Four topology scales rather than a
continuous curve; single- and multi-hub graphs rather than irregular or
production topologies (\S\ref{sec:hrw}); and the weight sensitivity and
PPO-vs-DQN comparisons are scored structurally rather than by measured latency,
which is exact and hardware-independent but assumes the structure-to-latency
relationship established in Table~\ref{tab:backupload}.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Every NF state-replication system we surveyed specifies how state reaches another
host and leaves open which host it reaches. We closed that gap and found that the
harder problem is establishing the answer rather than producing one.

On a bare-metal testbed where 128 backups each hold a dedicated physical core and
degraded hosts are impaired by the kernel, feature-aware selection routes a
primary to a degraded backup in $0\%$ of calls at 16, 32 and 128 hosts, against a
$10$--$12.5\%$ base rate for ordering-based selection, roughly halving p99 with
$r = -1.000$ across paired runs. A multi-criteria greedy heuristic is
statistically indistinguishable from the exact capacity-constrained assignment at
every scale, so the exact solve is a useful bound rather than a necessary
deployment. The advantage is specific to heterogeneous pools: with identical
hosts no method separates from random, and the gap grows with the pool.

Three results concern how such a claim must be measured, and apply beyond this
system. A connection-per-call RPC stack with a short accept queue biases the
measured placement effect with a sign that depends on workload concentration:
holding everything else fixed, raising the queue length took a measured
concentration penalty from $7.5\times$ to $35\%$ at 128 hosts while \emph{widening}
the gap between placement methods at 16. Degrading a contiguous block of hosts
allows an ordering-based baseline to report perfect avoidance it did not earn,
which is why we report the share of each method's calls that met the impairment
alongside its latency. And re-solving the instantaneous optimum every round, the
natural way to deploy an exact one-shot method, has unbounded competitive ratio
once migration costs anything; the cost of migration itself is roughly $1/\delta$
rounds for a delta share $\delta$ of state per round, a property of traffic churn
that no choice of wire format changes.

On reinforcement learning we report what we measured. PPO matches the
feature-aware methods to 32 hosts and, at 128, learns degraded-host avoidance
completely while load-balancing less tightly; DQN reproduces the same
concentration under the same remedy. Host-quality discrimination and load
balancing are acquired at different rates as the action space grows, and a scalar
per-step penalty does not close the gap. Scoring an assignment as a whole rather
than one primary at a time is the more promising formulation.

Three directions follow. The selection should be driven by live orchestrator
telemetry rather than synthetic draws, which our sensitivity analysis already
argues for: a host degraded only in its network path defeats a scoring function
built on resource gauges until latency is measured rather than derived. The
online policies should be evaluated on the testbed rather than in simulation.
And a packet-level evaluation over a working SDN fabric would let the RPC-level
failure rate be cross-checked against DEFT's native packet-drop metric. The
harness, trained models, raw per-call records and the saved output of every run,
including the configurations that produce the measurement artifacts above, are
available for reproduction.
