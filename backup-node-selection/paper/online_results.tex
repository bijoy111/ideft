\subsection{What Online Adaptation Is Worth}
\label{sec:online:results}

We evaluate five policies against the exact offline optimum on 16 hosts, 10
primaries, horizon 300, over ten seeds, reporting excess over the unavoidable
cost floor so that the ratio measures decision quality rather than the floor
(Table~\ref{tab:online-churn}).

\paragraph{Snapshot-chasing fails exactly as predicted.} Theorem~\ref{thm:snapshot}
is not a worst-case curiosity. Driving the construction of its proof directly,
the measured ratio tracks $1/\epsilon$ over four decades: $5.9$, $59.4$, $594$
and $5940$ as $\epsilon$ falls from $10^{-1}$ to $10^{-4}$. On the churn traces
it is the worst policy at every setting, spending $65$--$86\%$ of its total cost
on migration.

\paragraph{Almost all of the gain comes from failover, not from degradation
tracking.} At the most volatile setting \textsc{static} reaches an excess ratio
of $42.25$: a policy that never moves eventually sits on dead replicas. Adding
failover alone --- move only when a host dies --- drops this to $4.64$, closing
$91.2\%$ of the distance to the offline optimum. Adding full degradation-aware
hysteresis on top takes it to $4.59$, a further $0.13$ percentage points. The
ordering is stable across churn rate, degradation duration and pool size.

We report this negative result as the main empirical finding of this section,
and note that it mirrors the one-shot case: \S\ref{sec:testbed} finds that where
hosts are equivalent no selection method separates from random, and here, where
the expensive signal is \emph{how} a host is degrading rather than \emph{whether}
it is dead, tracking it buys almost nothing. \textsc{static} is only this poor
because it sits on failures forever; failover, not \textsc{static}, is the
honest baseline, and it is the one we compare against.

\paragraph{The budget holds, and the threshold does not need tuning.}
Proposition~\ref{prop:budget} bounds migration spend at $1/\alpha$ of service
cost. It holds at every threshold measured, with the tightest observation at
roughly half the permitted spend. Ratio is best near $\alpha = 4$ and is
insensitive between $1$ and $8$, so an operator can fix a migration budget in
advance and the policy will respect it. Migrations take effect at $t+1$, not within the round that triggers them.
That is the physically correct semantics: a primary cannot retroactively hold
state on the new backup. It is also necessary for the bound. With same-round
effect a policy accumulates regret on a host and then avoids paying for it, and
the budget fails at $\alpha = 4$.
