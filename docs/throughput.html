Throughput and Failure Rate

The latency pages cover speed per call. This page adds the other two things DEFT's own paper (CNSM'23) measures: throughput and dropped packets. Here each primary sends calls as fast as it can for a fixed 3 seconds, rather than a fixed number of calls, and we count successful calls per second and the share that time out or fail even after a retry.

A note on names: this test works at the RPC level (TCP). It is not DEFT's own packet-level buffer overflow, which needs the Mininet path this test deliberately skips. "Failure rate" here points at the same underlying problem, a backup too overloaded to keep up, but it is not the same measurement as DEFT's packet drop.

Only 3 of the 21 pairwise comparisons reached significance in this run (all three involving heuristic, p∈[0.03,0.05], small effects), and draw no ordering from that: with only 15 aggregate measurements per method, this metric is dominated by background system load, not the mapping under test. The one consistent signal is failure rate — concentrated's 0.70% is the highest, while the feature-aware methods stay at 0.07–0.14%. The 16-host result below is the only informative throughput result.

The result that repeats most reliably on this site: concentrated is clearly worse than everything else (r between 0.63 and 0.78, every p < 0.004), and its failure rate (2.57%) is well above every other method's (0.16–0.62%). Throughput confirms the crowding problem directly, so it shows up here as well as in the slowest calls.
Being honest about what this cannot show: throughput only partly picks up the bad-host effect. optimal and rl both beat round_robin clearly (r = 0.63 and 0.65, p < 0.004), which fits round_robin sending 2 of its 10 primaries to a bad host in every seed. But heuristic against round_robin is not clear enough to call (p = 0.38), even though heuristic avoids bad hosts just as well as the other two (see the 16-host latency page, where the effect is obvious for all three). The reason is simple: only 2 of round_robin's 10 primaries actually sit on a bad backup, and their lower throughput gets averaged in with the other 8 normal ones. That leaves a signal small enough for ordinary run-to-run noise to decide the answer. The slowest calls, which is where this effect really lives, get smoothed away by that averaging. That is why we treat latency as the main measure and use this page as supporting evidence rather than a replacement.
